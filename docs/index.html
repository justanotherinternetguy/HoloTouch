A hand is not a mouse

Most attempts at spatial control map your hand to the pointer and stop there: a few awkward gestures, laid on top of an interface built for a keyboard and mouse. HoloTouch starts from what hands already do.

Intuitive

Pinch to pick a window up. Make a fist to shut it. Turn a knob in the air for the volume. Gestures your hands already know.

Space first

It uses the room around you. Tip two fingers at the screen to scroll, or touch a fist to your chin and every window is laid out as a card.

Extensible

Replace what the pie menu holds, add macros you fingerspell, and record your own hands to teach it your alphabet.

Quick and consistent

Thirty frames a second on a laptop CPU, with an ordinary webcam. A slow hand does not jitter and a fast one does not lag.

The closest you will get to Tony Stark tossing windows around.

Fifteen gestures

Each is shown the way the control panel's guide shows it: a hand, and what it acts on.

Move a window

Thumb + index

Pinch over a window and carry it. A quick pinch brings it to the front.

Resize

Second hand pinches too

While one hand holds a window, pinch with the other and pull them apart.

Maximize or minimize

Flick a held window

Flick it up and let go to maximize it, down to minimize it.

Send to a workspace

Hold at a screen edge

Carry a window to the left or right edge of the screen and wait there.

Click

Point, thumb down

Point up, thumb out, then tap the thumb down onto your middle finger. Twice is a double click; keep it down to drag.

Pie menu

Thumb + pinky

Move toward an item and let go to pick it: apps, windows, music.

Close a window

Fist, held still

Hold a fist over the window until the ring completes.

Scroll

Two fingers up

Tip the two fingers at the screen to scroll down, straighten them up to scroll up: the further, the faster.

Volume and brightness

Claw, turned

Grip a knob in the air and turn it. Right hand: volume. Left: brightness.

Enter

Open palm, swept right

Sweep an open hand quickly to the right to press the Enter key.

Window switcher

Fist to chin

Every window becomes a card. Point at one and pinch to go to it.

Camera

Peace sign, both hands

Hold both up until the ring completes to open the camera and take a photo.

Dictate

Thumb and pinky out

Hold up the letter Y and speak. Let go, and what you said is typed where the keyboard is.

App launcher

Thumb, index and pinky out

Hold up I love you, then fingerspell an app's name until it is the only one left, or a macro's letters.

New tab

Clap twice

With a web browser in front, clap your hands twice to open a new tab in it.

Send to phone

Open palm, tossed up

With a web browser in front, hold an open hand still, then flick it straight up: the page opens on your phone.

Eleven hand shapes, read by rule

From 21 landmarks a hand, HoloTouch measures how far the thumb is from each fingertip and how curled each finger is, in palm lengths. Hand-written rules turn that into a pose.

An open palm. Swept fast to the right, it presses Enter.

The eleventh is a hand at rest, which does nothing. Each pose has separate thresholds for entering and leaving, and must hold for a moment before it counts, so a pose does not flicker.

An overlay that reads on anything

A see-through, click-through layer shows where your hands are and what each gesture is about to do. Every shape pairs cream with ink, so it reads over light and dark windows alike.

How it works

Three kinds of process: a tracker that finds hands, a core that turns them into gestures and acts on windows, and an optional control panel that starts, stops and watches the core.

  1. 1

    Tracker

    Camera to landmarks

    The webcam is read at 1280×720, 30 frames a second. MediaPipe finds up to two hands, 21 landmarks each, and a face mesh for the chin. It runs in a process of its own, so inference never stalls the overlay.

  2. 2

    Core

    Landmarks to gestures

    Each hand keeps its identity when hands cross. A 1€ filter smooths a slow hand hard and a fast hand lightly. The engine starts the matching interaction, and one runs at a time.

  3. 3

    Desktop

    Gestures to windows

    xfwm4's windows are changed with standard EWMH requests. Clicks and keys go through XTEST, and scrolling through a virtual mouse for smooth wheel events.

  4. 4

    Overlay

    What you see

    A fullscreen, transparent Qt Quick window sits above everything and draws the cursors, frames, menus and rings. Nothing samples or blurs the desktop.

Misread hands do no damage

  • A hand must be in view and relaxed for a moment before its gestures count.
  • A pinch that begins while the hand is moving fast is ignored.
  • A grab shorter than 300 ms is undone.
  • Only application windows can be changed, never the desktop or a panel.

Get started

HoloTouch runs on a laptop CPU with an ordinary webcam.

What it needs

  • Linux with an X11 session, xfwm4 and a running compositor
  • A webcam
  • Python 3.12

A few tools are optional, each for one feature: smooth scrolling, volume, dictation and the rest. holotouch doctor reports which are present.

Terminal 
$ git clone https://github.com/justanotherinternetguy/HoloTouch
$ cd HoloTouch
$ uv sync
$ uv run holotouch doctor
$ uv run holotouch panel
holotouch panel
Open the control panel: start and stop, the gesture guide, and practice.
holotouch run
Start tracking and the overlay.
holotouch run --dry-run
Practice on two stand-in windows. Your real ones are left alone.
holotouch doctor
Check the machine, and measure the tracking rate.
